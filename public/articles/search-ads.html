Every sponsored slot you have ever scrolled past was won in an auction that most advertisers do not understand the entrance to.

Quick-commerce apps sell those slots the same way Amazon and Flipkart do: a brand names the searches it wants to appear in, names a price per click, and the platform runs an auction the instant you hit search. Simple enough to explain in a sentence — which is why almost everyone gets the hard part wrong, and why the hard part isn't the price. It starts one step earlier, with a dairy brand and a spreadsheet.

A dairy brand put two lakh rupees behind curd. Good SKUs, real budget, sensible keywords: curd, yogurt, fresh curd, probiotic curd. Every one of them defensible in a review meeting. Every one of them written by someone who thinks in category names, because that is what the catalogue taught them to think in.

The campaign underperformed, and the post‑mortem went where post-mortems always go — the bid, the creative, the pacing, the hour of day. None of it was the problem. Tap the button and watch the actual problem arrive.

Twenty-seven percent. The brand paid full price to compete for roughly a quarter of its own market, and every rupee of optimisation after that point was spent making a quarter of the market slightly more efficient.

The misconception

Bidding is the loud part

Bidding feels like the hard problem because bidding is where the money visibly moves. You can watch it. You can tune it at 11pm. It produces a chart.

But a bid only decides how hard you compete in auctions you are already in. Keywords decide which auctions exist for you at all. A perfect bid on a keyword nobody types is a perfect bid on nothing.

So the real question at campaign setup was never "how much per click". It is: what is the actual vocabulary of demand for this product, in this city, this week? That question is harder than it sounds, and it fails in three distinct ways.

  • The vocabulary gap. Every category has a written name and a spoken one, and they are rarely the same word. Dahi and curd. Sooji and semolina. Meetha and dessert.
  • The cold start. A new SKU lands on Monday with no clicks, no orders, no query history. Every data-driven recommender is looking for signals that do not exist yet, so the product that most needs good keywords gets the worst ones.
  • The unstated goal. Two campaigns can look identical and want opposite things — steal a rival's traffic, or stop a rival stealing yours. Nobody writes it down anywhere a system can read.

Discovery · part one

Let the clicks name the product

The first source of truth is behaviour, and it doesn't require anyone to understand any words at all. Build a bipartite graph: search queries on one side, products on the other, an edge every time a search led to a click or an add-to-cart, weighted by how often it happened.

Then walk it. Drag the slider and watch what each extra hop buys you.

Hop 1 — the obvious wordsThe ones the brand already wrote down. No new money here.

Why the walk has to stop. Traversal is greedy. Push far enough and everything connects to everything — this is the whole reason edge weights need a floor that moves with the data instead of a constant somebody picked in a meeting.

This is powerful precisely because it is illiterate. The graph surfaces dahi for your curd because a few hundred thousand people demonstrated the link, not because anyone taught it Hindi. It will surface regional terms, misspellings, transliterations and competitor names with equal indifference.

The failure mode is on the other side. Walk one hop too far and you are recommending bread for a face wash, because somewhere in the middle a single basket contained both. Most of the real engineering here isn't the traversal — it's the governor on it.

Discovery · part two

The day-zero problem

The graph has one fatal gap: it only knows what already happened. A brand launching Organic Quinoa Cookies on Monday has zero edges, so the traversal returns nothing and the recommender shrugs at exactly the moment the advertiser needs it most.

So you stop asking what people did, and start asking what things mean.

What's actually happening. A sentence encoder fine-tuned on your own query and product history puts both into one vector space. Keyword discovery becomes a nearest-neighbour lookup that works on day zero, with no clicks at all.

Behaviour tells you what people did. Meaning tells you what is plausible when nobody has done anything yet. Neither is sufficient — an honest system routes between them based on how much history a product actually has, and stops trusting the encoder the moment real edges show up.

Matching

How wide should the net be?

Finding the keyword is half the recommendation. The other half is how strictly it should fire — and that choice is worth more money than most bid tuning.

The trap is phrase match. It matches letters, not intent — which is how "dahi handi decoration" ends up spending a dairy brand's budget in August.

Broad match is the only mode that catches curd from dahi, and it is also the mode that catches recipe browsers who will never add anything to a cart. Which is fine — as long as the system knows what the advertiser is trying to do. It usually doesn't, because nobody tells it.

Intent

Reading a plan nobody wrote down

A campaign defending its own brand and a campaign raiding a competitor's traffic look nearly identical in the database. But a recommendation that is brilliant for one is actively wasteful for the other, so the system has to guess — and advertisers leave fingerprints everywhere.

Negatives are the loudest signal. Excluding a term is a deliberate act in a way that including one never is — nobody adds a negative keyword by accident.

Most of this is rules, and the rules are unglamorous: tag each keyword branded or generic with token and trigram matching, count the mix, read the negatives, look at the match-type distribution. Heavy exact means control. Heavy broad means someone is hunting for customers they don't have yet.

The rules break in exactly one place, and it breaks in both directions. Some keywords are branded without looking it — chyawanprash reads like a category, but if ninety percent of its clicks land on one manufacturer it is functionally a brand term, and treating it as generic hands a rival cheap access to someone's shelf. Others look branded and aren't: nobody typing maggi at 1am is expressing brand loyalty, they are typing the word for instant noodles. Classify that as brand defence and you will hand the advertiser exactly the wrong recommendation.

No token match survives that. What separates the two cases isn't spelling, it's the click distribution — and reading it needs a model that can hold both the word and the behaviour at once. That tail is where a language model earns its seat: few-shot, at the end of the pipeline, on the ten percent the rules already gave up on.

Scope discipline The LLM does not run this system. It handles the fuzzy ten percent that deterministic rules can't reach, after the rules have done the cheap work. That distinction is the difference between a pipeline you can debug and one you can only pray at.

Bidding

Now you can talk about money

Once you know the word, the product and the goal, a bid stops being a guess. It becomes an optimisation with a shape you can actually see — and the shape is not the one most advertisers assume.

The peakEvery extra rupee here still buys clicks that convert.

Assumptions on the table. Conversion 9%, average order ₹240 — so a click is worth ₹21.60 and that is where you stop breaking even. Change those two numbers and the peak moves — which is the entire point of modelling it per category instead of publishing one number.

Three questions stack up inside that curve. What is the category worth — price point, baseline conversion, how many advertisers are fighting over it? How well does this specific SKU convert, given a best-seller turns the same click into an order more often than a long-tail item? And where does the return stop paying for the spend?

Note what the peak is not. It is not maximum reach, and it is not maximum return on ad spend — RoAS is highest at a bid so low you barely appear. The peak is where extra visibility still pays for itself, which is a target no advertiser states and every advertiser wants.

What holds up

Two things I'd tell anyone building this

Start rule-based, and don't be embarrassed about it. A first version that is eighty percent heuristics beats manual campaign setup, because manual keyword lists are far worse than anyone admits in a QBR. Let machine learning replace the rules only where you can point at the failure. Sophistication before data is just expensive guessing with extra steps.

Relevance is not a tax on revenue. Better targeting means the shopper sees the thing they actually wanted, which lifts click-through, which lifts return on ad spend, which brings the advertiser back with a bigger budget. The user-experience argument and the money argument point the same direction here, which almost never happens anywhere else in monetisation — so when it does, use it.

The brand in the opening did eventually find the word. It took a graph walk that nobody on the marketing team asked for, and it cost them a quarter of a year of spend to discover something four hundred million people already knew.

Spend the time on vocabulary. It's the unglamorous half. It's also where the money is.
